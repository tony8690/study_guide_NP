On-premises connectivity

There are three broad ways to connect an on-premises network to OCI, and most real designs combine at least two of them. FastConnect gives you a dedicated, private physical connection with predictable bandwidth and latency — the right choice when you're moving large or steady volumes of traffic and need a stable SLA. Site-to-Site IPSec VPN runs over the public internet instead, so it's faster to provision and cheaper, but latency and throughput are less predictable — it's commonly deployed as a backup path, or as the primary path for smaller sites that don't justify a dedicated circuit. Multicloud interconnects (like the Oracle-Azure Interconnect) are a specialized third category, purpose-built for connecting directly to another cloud provider rather than to a physical on-premises site.

Whichever combination you choose, all of them terminate on a Dynamic Routing Gateway (DRG) attached to your VCN, and all of them require your on-premises CIDR ranges to not overlap with your OCI CIDR ranges. When you run two paths for redundancy, BGP's AS-path length is your main lever for controlling which one is preferred: the shorter path wins, so you prepend extra AS hops onto whichever path should be the backup.

All three paths terminate on the same DRG, and all require non-overlapping CIDRs on both sides — the DRG is what makes them interchangeable from the VCN's point of view.

FastConnect

FastConnect virtual circuits come in two flavors that are easy to mix up on the exam. A private peering virtual circuit reaches your actual VCN resources — it attaches to a DRG just like a VPN does. A public peering virtual circuit instead reaches Oracle's public services (Object Storage, public load balancer endpoints, and so on) over a dedicated connection instead of the public internet — useful when you want the reliability of a private link without exposing anything inside a VCN. Because public peering involves advertising your own public IP prefixes to Oracle, Oracle has to verify you actually own those prefixes before it will advertise OCI's public IPs back to you — a process that's documented as taking up to three business days, which is exactly the kind of detail exam questions like to test.

Physically, you can establish FastConnect three ways: by colocating your own equipment in an Oracle FastConnect location, through a FastConnect partner, or through a third-party provider network. For redundancy, cross-connects can be bundled into a Cross-Connect Group. BGP prefix limits differ by circuit type and are a favorite "why is BGP down" scenario: a private virtual circuit tops out at 2,000 IPv4 prefixes (500 for IPv6), while a public virtual circuit's limit is much lower at just 200 prefixes — advertise past either limit and OCI brings the BGP session down for 60 minutes, not just until you fix it.

Site-to-Site VPN

An OCI Site-to-Site VPN pairs a CPE object (representing your on-premises device) with an IPSec connection attached to a DRG. Each IPSec connection actually gives you two tunnels by default, terminating on different infrastructure, purely for redundancy — that's independent of whether you're also running a second VPN or FastConnect circuit for site-level redundancy. You can route over the tunnel statically (explicit CIDRs on both ends) or dynamically with BGP, and the choice usually comes down to whether your on-premises router supports BGP at all.

The most commonly tested quirk is what OCI's BGP implementation does to the AS path: it prepends its own ASN to routes it receives, so if you advertise a route with an AS path length of 1, OCI's side will see it as length 2. If you ever see that exact "+1" pattern in a scenario, it's telling you the connection is a BGP-based IPSec VPN — not FastConnect, not a static VPN.

A specialized variant, IPsec over FastConnect, exists for when you want the low, predictable latency of a dedicated circuit but still need encryption in transit (private peering FastConnect alone is not encrypted). Instead of a normal public CPE, you create a private CPE object attached via a loopback DRG attachment type, which maps specific IPSec tunnels onto specific FastConnect virtual circuits rather than routing them over the internet. Because each tunnel individually caps out well below a full circuit's bandwidth, OCI supports ECMP across up to 8 tunnels on one connection so you can scale total encrypted throughput up to what the underlying FastConnect circuit can deliver.

Remote peering & transit routing

Local Peering Gateways only work within a single region. To connect VCNs — or to let on-premises reach a VCN — across two different regions, you need a Remote Peering Connection (RPC), which peers the two regions' DRGs directly over Oracle's backbone. RPC traffic isn't encrypted by default, so if that's a requirement, you need to layer encryption on top at the application or IPSec level. One hard boundary: RPC can only connect regions that sit inside the same OCI Realm (the commercial realm, OC1, is what most designs live in) — every region belongs to exactly one realm, and a realm with only a single region, or a standalone commercial region outside the OC1 realm, has no Inter-Region Latency data or RPC targets available at all.

The concept that trips people up most is transit routing: getting traffic to flow all the way from on-premises, through one region's DRG, across an RPC, into a second region's DRG, and finally into that region's VCN — and back again. This doesn't happen automatically just because the physical/logical connections exist. Each DRG route table only learns what its import route distribution tells it to import. So for two-way transit you need distributions configured in both directions: import the on-premises-facing attachment's routes into the RPC's DRG route table, and import the RPC attachment's routes into the on-premises-facing attachment's DRG route table — and mirror that same pair of distributions on the DRG in the second region.

Multicloud interconnects

Interconnects like the Oracle-Azure Interconnect exist for a specific scenario: an application tier running in one cloud that needs low-latency access to a database or service running in OCI (or vice versa), without routing everything through on-premises or the public internet. Architecturally it looks similar to FastConnect — a DRG on the OCI side peers with a Virtual Network Gateway or ExpressRoute circuit on the Azure side — but it's only available between specific paired regions that Oracle and the partner cloud have jointly built out, and it comes with a published, guaranteed round-trip latency under 2 ms (independent real-world testing has measured averages around 1.5 ms) rather than the "it depends on your region and AD" latency you get with ordinary FastConnect. Oracle-Azure was Oracle's first multicloud interconnect.

Oracle Interconnect for Google Cloud follows the same architectural idea, pairing a DRG with a GCP Cloud Router over a GCP Partner Interconnect circuit — still FastConnect underneath on the OCI side, still built for a split application/database workload across the two clouds.

Oracle Interconnect for AWS is the newest of the three (limited availability at launch) and breaks the pattern in one meaningful way: it's the only one of the three that can be initiated from either side — you can start the request from the OCI console or from AWS's Direct Connect console (look for "AWS Interconnect (multicloud)" there). Whichever side starts it, the other side has to accept and exchange a service key before BGP comes up; the finished connection reports independently on each console (OCI shows the virtual circuit as Provisioned with BGP up, AWS shows the connection as Available), with built-in IPv4 and IPv6 BGP redundancy validated on both ends. A common but non-ideal fallback some designs still reach for is standing up VPN software on a VM to tunnel from OCI to AWS — Oracle's best-practice guidance is explicit that whenever a native interconnect service is available, using it beats building and maintaining your own VM-based tunnel.

Azure and Google Cloud interconnects are always OCI-initiated; AWS is the exception and can be started from either cloud console.

Dynamic routing gateway

The DRG is the piece that ties everything together: it's the single resource that VCN attachments, IPSec VPN attachments, FastConnect virtual circuit attachments, and RPC attachments all connect to. But attaching two things to the same DRG doesn't automatically mean they can route to each other — that's controlled entirely by DRG route tables and the import route distributions associated with each attachment. If you don't configure anything custom, OCI's auto-generated defaults let every attachment learn routes from every other attachment (a full mesh), which is fine for simple designs but wrong for anything that needs isolation.

The classic exam scenario is hub-and-spoke isolation: you want spoke VCN A and spoke VCN B to both reach a shared hub, but not to reach each other directly through the DRG. You solve this with a custom DRG route table per spoke attachment, where the import route distribution's match criteria excludes routes coming from other spoke attachments. The one detail worth memorizing cold: DRG route tables never learn routes directly from a BGP session or from the attached resource itself — everything flows through an explicit import route distribution.

A DRG route table entry can also show a Conflict status: this happens when two IPSec tunnel (or virtual circuit) attachments both advertise the same on-premises route with the same AS path length, so OCI can't automatically prefer one over the other. By default the conflicting route sits inactive and only the first-learned tunnel actually carries traffic — the fix is enabling ECMP on the DRG route table, which tells OCI to treat both equal-cost routes as active and load-share across them (and BGP-derived routes with a populated AS path are what can conflict this way; VCN/static routes, which always have an empty AS path, aren't part of this conflict resolution).

Virtual cloud network design

A VCN is really just a CIDR block plus the plumbing you attach to it: subnets, route tables, and security rules. Subnets in modern OCI are regional by default — spanning every availability domain in the region — rather than tied to a single AD, which simplifies high-availability design. Traffic between two subnets in the same VCN is always allowed to route (that's implicit, no route table entry needed); what actually gates it is your security lists and NSGs. A VCN's original CIDR can't be changed once subnets depend on it, though you can add additional CIDR blocks alongside it as you grow.

Two features worth knowing cold for the exam: VCN Flow Logs capture traffic metadata (source, destination, ports, accept/reject) for auditing and troubleshooting without capturing payload, and OCI supports dual-stack IPv4/IPv6 VCNs. For IPv6 subnetting math, remember the rule of thumb — going from a /56 VCN allocation to /64 subnets uses 8 bits, so 2^8 = 256 possible subnets, and that pattern scales the same way for any GUA-to-subnet size difference.

Every instance connects through a VNIC (Virtual Network Interface Card), which always gets a private IP from its subnet's CIDR; a VNIC in a public subnet can additionally be assigned a public IP. DHCP Options is a small but real VCN-level resource attached to each subnet — since OCI runs DHCP for you, the only things you actually configure are the DNS type (the built-in Internet and VCN Resolver, or a custom DNS server IP you specify) and the DNS search domain instances should use.

Every VNIC gets a private IP from its subnet's CIDR regardless of type — only a public subnet's VNICs are eligible for a public IP on top of that.

IP address management

OCI gives you more control over addressing than "just pick a CIDR," and the exam draws a sharp line between the ephemeral and reserved lifecycle for both public and private IPs. An Ephemeral Public IP is tied to the VNIC that requested it — exactly one per VNIC, and it's released permanently the moment that VNIC or instance terminates; you cannot convert it into something persistent after the fact. A Reserved Public IP, by contrast, is its own standalone resource with an independent lifecycle: you can create it before you need it, attach it to an instance, detach it, and reattach it to a completely different instance later — the address survives instance termination. The identical pattern exists on the private side with Reserved Private IPs, which is how you build a "floating IP" that fails over between two instances in an HA pair.

IP Pools let you group a range of addresses — either Oracle-assigned or a BYOIP CIDR you've delegated to Oracle — into a named pool that Reserved Public IPs are then drawn from, which keeps a large deployment's addressing consistent, auditable, and easier to reason about for IP reputation purposes. Separately, because a subnet can hold more than one CIDR block, a single VNIC can be assigned secondary private IPs (IP CIDR Addresses for VNICs) out of any of those blocks — not just its primary one — a pattern used for hosting multiple containers or logical services behind one VNIC.

DNS: public, private & traffic steering

OCI Public DNS is authoritative for zones you delegate to it, and answers are served from Oracle's IP-anycast global edge rather than a single region. Private DNS solves a different problem: resolving names for resources that live inside your VCNs. It's built from views (a resolution scope), private zones attached to a view, and a resolver that every VCN gets for free at the reserved address 169.254.169.254. You can attach a resolver to multiple VCNs so they share one private view, and a forwarding rule on that view can hand off specific domains to an external or on-premises DNS server (or accept forwarded queries coming the other way) — the standard pattern for hybrid environments where some names live on-prem and some live in OCI.

Traffic Management steering policies let a single DNS record return different answers depending on who's asking or what's healthy, evaluated against configurable health checks: load balancer/ratio policies split traffic by weighted percentage (the mechanism behind gradual, DNS-based migrations); failover policies return a backup answer only when the primary is unhealthy; geolocation and ASN policies answer based on the query's geographic origin or originating network; and IP-prefix policies key off the querying resolver's CIDR block.

DNSSEC is the other area worth memorizing precisely, because the two keys have different owners: the Key-Signing Key (KSK) is customer-managed, while the Zone-Signing Key (ZSK) is Oracle-managed. Together they produce the DNSKEY, RRSIG, DS, and NSEC records a validating resolver uses to confirm a zone's answers haven't been tampered with. The detail that catches people out: DNSSEC is only supported on public zones, never private zones — validation depends on every parent zone in the hierarchy up to the top-level domain also being signed, and with a private zone you don't control that whole chain, so there's nothing to validate against. Migrating an already-signed zone into OCI follows a strict order — disable DNSSEC on the source zone, copy the records over, then enable DNSSEC on the OCI zone — and a secondary zone can only use DNSSEC if the primary DNS provider has it enabled too. One more combination to rule out: DNSSEC and secondary egress can't coexist on the same zone, though secondary ingress from an external DNSSEC-signed provider into an OCI zone works fine, with the external provider staying responsible for signing.

Internet & NAT gateways

An Internet Gateway (IGW) is what makes a subnet "public": it lets resources with a public IP send and receive traffic directly with the internet, provided the subnet's route table sends internet-bound traffic to it and security rules allow it. It doesn't do any address translation — it's a straight pipe. A NAT Gateway solves the opposite problem: it lets instances in a private subnet (no public IP at all) reach the internet outbound, while blocking anything from initiating a connection inbound. The NAT Gateway itself holds the public IP and translates on the instances' behalf. A single subnet's route table can mix targets — a specific CIDR routed to a DRG alongside a catch-all 0.0.0.0/0 routed to the NAT Gateway, for example — since OCI evaluates the most specific matching route rule.

Service gateway, private endpoints & PSA

A Service Gateway lets resources in a private subnet reach specific Oracle services — Object Storage being the classic example — without a public IP, a NAT Gateway, or the internet at all. It's regional and attached to a whole VCN: one gateway serves every subnet and every supported service at once, via a route rule that points the "Oracle Services Network" CIDR label at the gateway. The catch is that this traffic still resolves to Oracle's own public service IP ranges — it just never leaves Oracle's network to get there. The detail that shows up constantly on practice exams: Service Gateway is strictly one-directional. It only carries traffic that your VCN initiates outward toward Oracle's services — an Oracle service can never use it to initiate a connection back into your VCN.

A Private Endpoint takes a different, per-resource approach: it gives one specific service resource — a single Autonomous Database, or Object Storage scoped to particular buckets, namespaces, or compartments — its own dedicated private IP and FQDN inside a subnet you choose. It's granular but not shared: provision five Autonomous Databases with private access and you get five separate private endpoints, each with its own address. Some services also build on this for a Reverse Connection Endpoint (RCE) — a narrower capability that lets a supported Oracle service initiate a secure connection back into your network, the one case where traffic flows in the opposite direction from a Service Gateway.

Private Service Access (PSA) is a third, separate resource type — not just an umbrella term covering the other two. A PSA endpoint is its own VNIC with one dedicated private IP and FQDN per service API, shared across every resource of that type: a single PSA endpoint covers all five of those Autonomous Databases, the way a Service Gateway covers a whole region's services, but addressed with a private IP instead of a shared public CIDR label. It supports IPv4-only or dual-stack IPv4/IPv6 networks, and access is controlled exactly like any other VNIC — security lists, NSGs, or Zero Trust Packet Routing policies. For Object Storage specifically, a PSA endpoint blocks cross-tenancy pre-authenticated requests, foreign-tenancy credentials, and anonymous access, and supports up to 25 Gbps of throughput — which is why Oracle recommends PSA as the default choice for most private Object Storage designs, reserving Private Endpoint for cases that need to restrict access to specific buckets, namespaces, or compartments for particular clients.

Only the Private Endpoint path (row 3) lets a supported Oracle service initiate the connection — Service Gateway and PSA traffic only ever flow outward from your VCN. The difference between row 2 and row 4 is the address: Service Gateway resolves to a shared public CIDR label, while a PSA endpoint is a dedicated private IP covering every resource of that one service.

Local peering gateway

An LPG peers exactly two VCNs, and only within the same region — that's the entire scope of what it does, which is also why it's such a clean contrast with RPC on the exam (same region → LPG, cross region → RPC). A VCN can have multiple LPGs to peer with multiple partner VCNs, each pair needing non-overlapping CIDRs. In hub-and-spoke designs built with LPGs rather than a DRG, the hub's LPG for each spoke can carry its own VCN route table, which is what actually determines how traffic arriving from that spoke gets routed once it's inside the hub.

Security lists & NSGs

Both security lists and network security groups (NSGs) are firewall rule sets, and OCI evaluates them together rather than one overriding the other — a packet is allowed through if any applicable rule, from either mechanism, permits it. The difference is scope: a security list's rules apply to every VNIC in the subnet it's attached to, while an NSG's rules apply only to the specific VNICs you've explicitly added to that NSG, regardless of which subnet they live in. That makes NSGs the better tool for microsegmentation — you can define a "web-tier" NSG and a "db-tier" NSG, write a rule that says "allow traffic from the web-tier NSG to the db-tier NSG" (referencing the NSG itself as the source, not a CIDR), and that rule keeps working correctly even as you add or move instances between subnets. Both mechanisms support stateful rules (return traffic auto-allowed) or stateless rules (both directions must be explicit). One limit worth remembering: an NSG-as-source reference only works within a single VCN — cross-VCN, the only native option is a CIDR-based rule, which is exactly the gap Cross-VCN ZPR (below) is designed to close.

Zero Trust Packet Routing

OCI Zero Trust Packet Routing (ZPR) is a different kind of security control from security lists, NSGs, or a firewall: instead of IP/CIDR/port rules tied to the network layout, you write intent-based policies in near-English, like "in the prod VCN, allow compute instances tagged app:science to connect to the database tagged database:sensitive on port 1521." The mechanics are two steps. First, you tag resources with security attributes — a namespace plus an attribute name and value, e.g. network:carconet for a VCN or app:tele-app for a compute instance. Second, you write a ZPR policy referencing those attributes; the policy is enforced at the packet layer, independent of whatever the route tables and security lists say.

The detail that matters most for the exam: the instant you attach a ZPR attribute to a resource, that resource flips to deny-by-default for ZPR purposes — even if a security list or NSG already permits the traffic, it's blocked until an explicit ZPR policy opens it. All three layers (security list/NSG, network firewall, ZPR) are then evaluated as a logical AND: a packet needs every applicable layer's blessing to get through, not just one. This is precisely how ZPR defends against the classic "someone tweaked a route table while fixing an unrelated issue and accidentally exposed the database to the internet" scenario — since ZPR doesn't care what the route table says, the database stays protected regardless.

Cross-VCN ZPR extends the same attribute model across VCN boundaries. In a hub-and-spoke design with a shared Network Firewall in the hub, NSGs and security lists still enforce port-level control at the edge, and the firewall still does SSL inspection, intrusion prevention, and URL filtering — but neither of those stops one application's load balancer in the hub from reaching a completely different application's compute instance in another spoke, because NSG references don't cross VCNs and CIDR-based cross-VCN rules are brittle. Tagging each app's resources with distinct attribute values and writing narrow Cross-VCN ZPR policies between only the matching pairs closes that lateral-movement gap without touching a single route table.

Network firewall & inline NVAs

When a design calls for inspecting traffic before it reaches an application — say, a third-party firewall appliance sitting in-path between the Internet Gateway and the applications subnet — the firewall typically performs source NAT (SNAT) on inbound traffic before forwarding it on, so the backend sees the firewall's IP rather than the original client's. That centralization is powerful but has real limits: a single appliance handling SNAT for every session can run out of NAT table entries or source ports under heavy concurrent load, and throughput can degrade as one box tries to inspect everything. What's not usually a limiting factor is interface count — a basic two-arm design (one interface facing the untrusted side, one facing the trusted side) is within reach of virtually any firewall appliance. To scale beyond one appliance's capacity, you distribute load across multiple firewall instances using a load balancer or ECMP routing. If you'd rather not operate a third-party appliance at all, OCI's own Network Firewall service — powered by Palo Alto Networks but fully OCI-managed — offers a managed, cloud-native alternative you deploy straight into a VCN. Its security rules use a classic 5-tuple (source/destination IP, source/destination port, protocol) and are evaluated top-to-bottom with first-match-wins, and URL-list rules must match the exact path — allowing oracle.com/yum won't also allow www.oracle.com. In hub-and-spoke topologies, centralizing this kind of firewall in the hub — with spoke-to-spoke traffic routed through it via DRG or LPG route tables — is a common way to get one inspection point for all inter-spoke traffic.

A newer capability, Tunnel Inspection (added late 2024), pairs the OCI Network Firewall with a VTAP: the VTAP mirrors VXLAN-encapsulated traffic to the firewall out-of-band, where it's decrypted and inspected for threats without touching the original flow's latency or throughput at all — this is detection and logging only, with no ability to block in real time, which is the fundamental trade-off of any out-of-band design versus deploying inline.

Web application firewall

OCI's Web Application Firewall (WAF) is a different animal from the Network Firewall: it's a Layer 7 reverse proxy purpose-built for HTTP(S) applications, PCI-compliant, and it sits in front of an origin that doesn't even have to be in OCI — on-premises and other-cloud origins are fully supported. There are two deployment flavors: the WAF Edge Policy, a global solution served from Oracle's points of presence worldwide and usable with any origin, and the regional WAF Policy, which attaches directly to an OCI Load Balancer listener or certificate. One hard limit to remember: WAF cannot protect a Network Load Balancer, because NLB is Layer 3/4 pass-through with no HTTP visibility for a Layer 7 proxy to inspect — WAF only works with the classic Load Balancer or the Edge policy's own listeners.

WAF's rule conditions are written in JMESPath, a query language that evaluates a JSON document describing the request or response — for example, a condition checking whether the request's country code equals a blocked value. Each rule pairs a condition with an action: Allow, Check (logs the match but doesn't stop rule processing, useful for tuning before enforcing), or Return HTTP Response (blocks the request or response and substitutes a custom reply). Beyond access control, WAF bundles rate limiting (per-IP request throttling against Layer 7 DDoS), bot management (JavaScript challenges, human-interaction checks, CAPTCHA, device fingerprinting, and a good-bot allowlist), protection rules against SQL injection/XSS/HTML injection sourced from OWASP guidance, and caching rules at the edge for eligible content. A Network Address List (NAL) is a separate, reusable object holding a set of trusted IPs/CIDRs — reference one in a rule to allowlist or blocklist a group of addresses (e.g. trusted partner ranges) without hand-writing a JMESPath condition per address. When the goal is specifically distinguishing bots from real users on a form like registration or checkout, CAPTCHA is the purpose-built tool — rate limiting caps volume regardless of who's asking, and a NAL only helps if you already know which addresses to trust or block.

Load balancer & NLB

OCI's classic Load Balancer operates at Layer 7, which is what lets it terminate SSL/TLS, inspect HTTP headers, and route based on URL path or host — the right tool when the application layer matters. The Network Load Balancer (NLB) instead operates at Layer 3/4: it's a pass-through, flow-based balancer that doesn't terminate SSL, but delivers much higher throughput and lower latency, and preserves the client's original source IP by default (useful for backend logging or security tooling that needs to see the real client). Both support backend sets spanning multiple availability domains and configurable health checks. On the CLI side, remember that --assigned-private-ipv4 always sets the private IP regardless of whether the resource ends up public or private — if --is-private No is set, the public IP comes from the reserved pool referenced by --reserved-ips, not from the assigned private IP value.

NLB enhancements loosened an early restriction: backends no longer have to live in the NLB's own VCN — they can sit in a different VCN, a different region, or even on-premises, as long as the underlying routing (LPG, DRG, RPC, FastConnect/VPN) actually gets packets there. Health checks apply to both balancer types and can run over TCP or HTTP, on a configurable interval, timeout, and retry count; the moment a backend fails its check it's pulled out of rotation without any manual intervention. The same idea extends into DNS: Traffic Management steering policies use their own health checks to detect an unhealthy endpoint and stop returning it in DNS answers, which is what makes a failover steering policy actually failover automatically.

Certificates & TLS

Every HTTPS connection starts with a TLS handshake: the client proposes supported TLS versions and cipher suites, the server responds with its certificate (public key plus identity information), the client verifies that certificate chains back to a trusted Certificate Authority (CA) and hasn't expired, and then a session key is generated to encrypt the rest of the conversation. A mutual TLS (mTLS) connection goes further, requiring both sides to present and verify a certificate — stronger authentication, at the cost of more compute and coordination.

OCI Certificates lets you run all of this internally at no extra charge: create a private root CA, issue subordinate (child) CAs from it, and issue leaf certificates from those — a chain of trust that a client walks all the way back to the root to establish validity. You're not limited to Oracle-issued certificates either — you can import a certificate from a public, trusted third-party CA (like the classic "buy from GoDaddy" flow) and use it interchangeably with an OCI-issued one on a Load Balancer or API Gateway. Two lifecycle states get confused constantly: an expired certificate simply reached its natural end date, while a revoked certificate had its trust pulled early — typically because a private key was compromised — and appears on a Certificate Revocation List (CRL). OCI also supports automatic renewal rules ahead of expiration and won't let you accidentally delete a certificate that's actively attached to a resource without a scheduled deletion window first.

Bastion

OCI Bastion exists to remove the need for a self-managed jump host: it's a fully managed, free service that brokers short-lived SSH or RDP sessions into private resources, with no public IP address of its own. There are three session types: managed SSH sessions (for compute instances running the Bastion cloud agent/plugin — which means the instance needs outbound access to the Oracle Services Network, typically via a Service Gateway, to actually fetch that agent), SSH/RDP port-forwarding sessions (1-to-1 to a specific host, no agent required), and dynamic port-forwarding sessions (covering an entire target subnet rather than a single host, still agent-free). Sessions require an Ed25519 SSH key specifically — generating the key through Bastion's own workflow is the safest way to guarantee this. Per tenancy (per region) you can have up to 5 Bastions, each supporting up to 20 concurrent sessions, and each VCN needs its own Bastion resource.

The mechanic worth internalizing is where traffic actually comes from once a session is active: the target resource sees the connection arriving from Bastion's own private endpoint IP, not from the original client's IP address. That means the target's security list or NSG should be scoped to allow the relevant port from that endpoint IP — opening the port to your entire corporate CIDR instead would defeat the purpose of routing through Bastion at all. Sessions also aren't permanent; each one lasts a minimum of 30 minutes up to a maximum time-to-live of 3 hours (180 minutes), after which it expires and you create a new one. Note that this is a different mechanism from Cloud Shell's ephemeral network, which only reaches resources in your tenancy's home region — Bastion has no such region restriction.

Cloud Shell

Cloud Shell is not a resource you create on a VCN — it's a free, browser-based Linux terminal embedded directly in the OCI Console, already authenticated with your console session and pre-loaded with the CLI, Ansible, and Python. Because it inherits your console identity, its permissions are a strict one-to-one mirror of your existing IAM policies — Cloud Shell never grants access beyond what you could already do. Under the hood, Cloud Shell itself runs on Oracle Linux 8, so its package manager is yum (Oracle Linux's Red Hat-family package manager) — if a question asks what command installs additional software inside Cloud Shell, that's the detail it's testing.

There are three network modes. Public reaches only resources that already have a public IP — no gateway or VCN configuration needed on Cloud Shell's side at all. Private creates a private endpoint on a subnet you choose and lets you save that configuration as a reusable, nameable template you can reactivate later. Ephemeral does the same thing but temporarily — the endpoint is discarded the moment the session ends. Both private modes share one restriction worth memorizing: they only work in your tenancy's home region. To reach a resource in another region, you SSH from Cloud Shell into a compute instance that itself already has cross-region connectivity (for example, over a Remote Peering Connection), and continue from there. One more quirk: Cloud Shell blocks the ping command outright — if you need ICMP reachability testing, you do it from inside a compute instance you've SSH'd into, not from Cloud Shell directly.

VTAP

A Virtual Test Access Point mirrors a copy of traffic to a chosen destination without touching the original flow's performance or path — useful for security monitoring, deep troubleshooting, or compliance capture. Configuring one always comes down to three pieces: a capture filter defining which traffic qualifies (by protocol, port, or direction), a source naming the resource being observed (typically a VNIC, but also load balancers, DB systems, Exadata VM clusters, and Autonomous Data Warehouse), and a target naming where the mirrored copy goes. The target has hard requirements worth memorizing: it must be an OCI Network Load Balancer with a UDP listener on port 4789 — the destination port for the VXLAN tunnel that carries mirrored traffic — and it must live in the same VCN as the source, though it's fine for it to sit in a different compartment. By default VTAP mirrors full jumbo frames up to 9,000 bytes, but you can configure a max packet size to truncate to headers only if your analysis tool can't handle full packets. Because mirrored traffic competes for the source VNIC's bandwidth, OCI drops it first under congestion by default — enabling VTAP priority mode gives mirrored traffic equal priority with production traffic, which matters if a compliance requirement mandates you never miss capturing specific flows.

VCN flow logs

VCN Flow Logs answer a narrower question than VTAP: not "what does this traffic actually look like," but "did this connection happen, and was it accepted or rejected." Each log entry is 5-tuple metadata — source/destination IP, source/destination port, protocol — plus the accept/reject decision, with no payload at all, delivered through the OCI Logging service where it can be searched, filtered, exported, or streamed to a third-party SIEM. That makes Flow Logs the lighter-weight, cheaper-to-run sibling of VTAP: reach for Flow Logs when you need to know whether traffic got through and why, and reach for VTAP when you need to actually inspect the packets themselves.

Flow Logs share the same capture-filter mechanics as VTAP — up to 10 ordered include/exclude rules, first match wins, so reordering rules changes behavior — plus a sampling rate to capture only a percentage of matching traffic and control cost. Enablement points control scope at three levels: VCN-wide (every current and future subnet and VNIC), subnet-level, or resource-level (a specific instance VNIC or load balancer) for the most targeted capture. The classic troubleshooting pattern: SSH access to an instance suddenly stops working, you enable a subnet-level flow log filtered to destination port 22, and a stream of "reject" entries immediately points you at a missing ingress rule in the security list — exactly the kind of gap a Flow Log finds faster than guessing.

Network Command Center tools

Three more Network Command Center tools round out OCI's built-in observability, and all three are free. Network Visualizer renders your actual deployed topology from live configuration — a regional topology map showing VCNs, DRGs, CPEs, and gateways; a VCN topology map showing subnets and route/security maps; and a subnet topology map down to individual VNICs — with click-through detail at every level. Inter-Region Latency is a dashboard of round-trip time (a rolling 5-minute average, refreshed every minute) between every pair of regions in the OCI commercial realm, useful both for planning a multi-region design before you build it and for confirming a suspicious ping result against baseline during troubleshooting.

Network Path Analyzer goes a step further than visualization: give it a source and destination (OCI-to-OCI, on-premises-to-OCI, or OCI-to-internet) and it performs a non-intrusive, simulated hop-by-hop reachability check — no real traffic is generated — analyzing up to 8 ECMP paths and pinpointing exactly which route rule or security rule is blocking a connection, or confirming a path is reachable when you expected it to fail. It has real limits worth knowing: it can't evaluate two resources in the same subnet (there's no route rule to analyze there), and it can't check LPG or RPC peering across different tenancies, IPv6 paths, or routing loops.

Workload migration

Moving workloads into OCI touches three separate problems: getting the data there, keeping IP dependencies intact, and cutting DNS over safely. For bulk data migration into Object Storage, OCI documents four paths: over the plain internet via HTTPS; over a FastConnect public-peering circuit, which reaches Oracle's public services over a dedicated line instead of the internet without ever touching a VCN's DRG; over FastConnect private peering, where the DRG's VCN route table sends Object-Storage-bound traffic to a Service Gateway (the DRG attachment alone doesn't reach the Oracle Services Network — you still need that Service Gateway route); or physically, via the Data Transfer Appliance, for one-time transfers at the petabyte scale where even a fast circuit would take too long.

Bring Your Own IP (BYOIP) solves the "hard-coded IP address" problem that trips up a lot of migrations: instead of re-numbering everything that points at your current public IPs, you delegate your own owned public CIDR to Oracle, which then advertises that exact range from OCI's edge — external consumers keep working against the same addresses, unaware anything moved, and OCI's IP-pool management helps preserve the range's reputation. Finally, DNS-based cutover uses a ratio-based (weighted) load balancing Traffic Management steering policy to shift a percentage of live traffic — say, 10% — from the existing on-premises deployment to a freshly stood-up OCI copy, then dials that percentage up as confidence grows, all without an all-or-nothing switch. As with any hybrid connectivity project, none of this works if the on-premises and OCI CIDR ranges overlap — that non-overlap requirement is a prerequisite before FastConnect or VPN can even be configured.

Networking best practices

Oracle's own best-practices guidance groups into four areas, and the exam draws individual facts from each. On design: do the networking design work early, before deployment, because some decisions are painful or impossible to reverse later — the standout example is that a private subnet can never have a public IP added to it, so guessing wrong on public-vs-private means rebuilding the subnet. Standardize naming conventions, choose subnet type and regional-vs-AD-specific scope deliberately, size CIDRs for future growth rather than just today's needs, and build custom route tables and security lists instead of leaving everything on the VCN's auto-generated defaults. A hub-and-spoke design is explicitly called out as the pattern to reach for once you need shared services, centralized security inspection, or isolation between multiple VCNs.

On security: Oracle recommends network security groups over security lists as the default choice, precisely because a security list's rules apply to every VNIC in the subnet — as a subnet grows, that quietly widens access far beyond what any individual workload needs, whereas an NSG only ever applies to the VNICs you explicitly add to it. Use the free managed OCI Bastion service instead of standing up and patching your own jump host, restrict who can touch networking resources with scoped IAM policies, and add OCI Network Firewall (or a third-party equivalent) where you need protocol-aware inspection that security lists and NSGs — which only understand ports — can't provide.

On connectivity: build full redundancy for any hybrid link, which specifically means validating that two FastConnect circuits terminate on physically different routers, not just that you have "two circuits." Prefer FastConnect over VPN as your primary path (FastConnect's dedicated bandwidth and predictable performance beat the public internet every time cost isn't the deciding factor), keep VPN as the backup path or as the primary only when cost sensitivity rules FastConnect out, test failover on a regular cadence, and lean on custom DRG route tables and import route distributions — including on any transit-routing design — rather than the defaults.

On monitoring, observability, and management: actually use the free Network Command Center tools (Visualizer, Path Analyzer, Inter-Region Latency) proactively as part of normal operations, not only when something breaks, since they're non-intrusive and cost nothing to try. Set up event notifications on your critical networking resources so configuration changes and outages surface immediately rather than being discovered by a user complaint.
